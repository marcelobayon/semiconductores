## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Indigenous Semiconductors is a proposed, long-horizon Latin American semiconductor ecosystem initiative. The initial geographic focus is Colombia and Peru, with the possibility of additional regional partners later.

The ambition is to build capabilities progressively across the semiconductor value chain:

1. Market intelligence, distribution, and customer relationships.
2. Research, education, and technical partnerships.
3. Proprietary semiconductor-related software and intellectual property.
4. Integrated-circuit design and prototyping.
5. Testing, reliability, assembly, and packaging capabilities.
6. Selective manufacturing capabilities if technically and commercially justified.
7. A sustainable regional supplier, talent, research, and customer ecosystem.

**Stochastic Cascade Systems (SECD)** is the core algorithm / technology brand being developed by Marcelo. It is intended to become one proprietary technology asset within the wider initiative. SECD is not synonymous with the entire semiconductor ecosystem and must not be represented as a complete chip-design or chip-manufacturing process.

The first practical work is research and validation, not construction of a fabrication plant.

### Strategic thesis

Latin America may be able to increase its participation in the global semiconductor value chain by building selected, commercially defensible capabilities rather than trying to reproduce the entire leading-edge semiconductor industry in one country.

Potential entry points include distribution, specialized component sourcing, semiconductor design, computational modeling, materials research, prototyping, testing, and selected packaging or manufacturing activities.

This thesis must be validated with actual customers, technical partners, market data, and feasibility studies.

## 5. THE SEMICONDUCTOR VALUE CHAIN — TARGET DEVELOPMENT MODEL

The initiative should understand the whole value chain while entering it in stages.

### A. Distribution and market access — potential first commercial wedge

Explore importing and distributing selected semiconductor components and related electronic parts to regional customers.

Potential activities:
- Identify high-demand component categories and applications.
- Map authorized manufacturers, franchised distributors, independent distributors, and local resellers.
- Understand minimum order quantities, lead times, payment terms, warranty, traceability, counterfeit risk, export controls, and import requirements.
- Interview electronics manufacturers, equipment integrators, industrial automation firms, mining-equipment suppliers, energy integrators, telecom suppliers, and agricultural technology companies.
- Determine whether customers face shortages, long lead times, high minimum orders, poor technical support, or other unmet needs.
- Test a narrow product category before building inventory.

Distribution is a market-learning and possible revenue strategy, not proof of a path to manufacturing. Do not purchase inventory before customer demand, supplier legitimacy, compliance, working-capital needs, and unit economics are validated.

### B. Research and computational technology

Develop SECD and other research tools that may support semiconductor-related modeling.

Potential areas to investigate:
- Stochastic lithography and defect modeling.
- Process variability and uncertainty quantification.
- Materials and device modeling.
- Reliability and failure analysis.
- Physics-informed computational methods.
- Simulation, optimization, and experimental-design tools.

Each application requires its own physics, data, validation, and benchmarks. Do not assume one model generalizes to every semiconductor process.

### C. Chip design and prototyping

Explore a fabless model: design proprietary chips and use established third-party foundries for initial fabrication.

Potential specialties to investigate:
- Power-management and power-electronics ICs.
- Sensors and mixed-signal ICs.
- Industrial control and interface ICs.
- Mining, energy, agricultural, and environmental monitoring applications.

Product selection must be driven by customer interviews and technical feasibility, not by a desire to manufacture a particular chip.

### D. Materials and process research

Investigate the actual materials and process requirements for semiconductor-grade inputs.

Do not assume that copper, silver, lithium, silicon-bearing minerals, or other mineral resources are automatically suitable semiconductor feedstock. Research:
- Required chemical and physical specifications.
- Purity and contamination thresholds.
- Refining and purification routes.
- Yield, energy, water, waste, and environmental requirements.
- Qualification standards and customer acceptance.
- Capital and operating costs.
- Existing global suppliers and competitive pricing.

Mining and mineral partners may provide valuable geology, extraction, processing, and supply-chain knowledge. Their role must be defined by specific material opportunities and evidence.

### E. Assembly, packaging, and testing

Investigate whether selected assembly, packaging, testing, and reliability capabilities could be developed regionally before considering wafer fabrication.

Potential capabilities:
- Chip packaging and interconnect.
- Wire bonding and related assembly processes.
- Electrical test and characterization.
- Reliability and failure analysis.
- Prototype and small-volume production support.

### F. Fabrication

A domestic wafer fabrication facility is a long-term possibility, not an assumed outcome.

Before any fab proposal, require:
- A defined process technology and product-market fit.
- Committed customers and credible demand forecasts.
- Capital and operating-cost model.
- Water, power, cleanroom, chemical, equipment, and waste-management feasibility.
- Equipment and materials supply chain.
- Workforce and process-engineering plan.
- Technology transfer and IP arrangements.
- Export-control and national-security review where relevant.
- Independent technical and financial feasibility studies.

No fab construction recommendation without a separate, evidence-backed investment case.

## 6. CUSTOMERS AND MARKET VALIDATION

### Initial customer hypotheses

Potential customer groups to investigate include:

1. **Electronics manufacturers and contract manufacturers** — integrated circuits and components incorporated into products.
2. **Industrial automation and control-system integrators** — microcontrollers, power devices, interface ICs, sensors, and related components.
3. **Mining-equipment manufacturers and maintenance providers** — sensors, monitoring electronics, control systems, power electronics, and ruggedized electronic modules.
4. **Energy and renewable-energy integrators** — power-management components, monitoring systems, inverters, and control electronics.
5. **Telecommunications and networking equipment suppliers** — signal, power, and communication components.
6. **Agricultural technology companies** — sensing, telemetry, control, and automation components.
7. **Universities and research laboratories** — research tools, prototyping, components, training, and collaborative projects.
8. **Distributors and electronics wholesalers** — potential channel partners or customers for selected product lines.

### Critical buyer distinction

Mining companies often buy complete equipment, systems, and maintenance services rather than buying individual chips directly. The direct buyer for a semiconductor may be the equipment OEM, electronics manufacturer, contract manufacturer, or systems integrator.

Research the full buying chain:
- End user.
- Equipment OEM.
- Electronics designer / manufacturer.
- Component distributor.
- Semiconductor manufacturer.
- Procurement decision-maker.
- Technical approver.

### First commercial research questions

- What components are being imported into Peru and Colombia?
- Which HS codes and product subcategories matter for the target application?
- Who are the largest buyers and importers?
- Which suppliers and distributors currently serve them?
- What are the recurring lead times, shortages, price changes, and minimum orders?
- Which products have enough demand and gross margin for a small distributor?
- What certifications, traceability, warranty, and technical support do buyers require?
- Would buyers consider locally designed alternatives?
- What volumes and qualification steps would be required for a new chip?
- Who signs the purchase order and who approves the technical design?

### Evidence required before choosing a product

For each candidate product, record:
- Named buyer or buyer category.
- Specific application and pain point.
- Existing component / part number or technical specification.
- Current source and price evidence.
- Annual or monthly volume estimate and source.
- Qualification and certification requirements.
- Expected gross margin and working capital.
- Competitive alternatives.
- Evidence of buyer willingness to test or purchase.
- Confidence level and next validation step.

Do not treat broad import totals, general industry growth, or expressions of interest as proof of addressable demand.

## 10. TEN-YEAR ROADMAP — PROVISIONAL

The timeline is an organizing framework, not a promise. Advancement depends on evidence and funding.

- Audit the SECD algorithm and repository.

- Obtain quotes and model working capital and unit economics.
- Identify a first customer problem for potential chip design.

### Phase 2 — Prototype and capability development (Years 1–3)

- Improve and benchmark SECD.
- Develop research datasets and reproducible experiments.
- Build design capability using established EDA workflows.
- Develop a prototype through an appropriate university, MPW, or foundry partner.
- Build materials and process research partnerships where a specific opportunity exists.
- Develop supplier quality and traceability practices.

**Gate:** Independently tested prototype or research result and a credible customer qualification path.

### Phase 3 — Commercial technology company (Years 3–5)

- Commercialize selected SECD software / IP if validated.
- Establish a fabless chip-design business if customer demand supports it.
- Develop repeat customers and supplier partnerships.
- Evaluate local test, reliability, assembly, and packaging services.
- Build a skilled engineering and operations team.

**Gate:** Revenue, repeat demand, quality systems, and unit economics justify expansion.

### Phase 4 — Selected domestic industrial capabilities (Years 5–10)

- Establish packaging, assembly, and testing capabilities if justified.
- Evaluate specialty process or mature-node fabrication only through an independent feasibility study.
- Develop qualified regional suppliers.
- Expand customer base and exports.
- Continue university, workforce, and international partnerships.

**Gate:** Invest only when customer commitments, infrastructure, technical leadership, financing, and risk controls support it.
